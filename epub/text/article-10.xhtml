15 Sep 2025

An American Man

By Zach Rabiroff

Author’s Note: the names in this article have been altered, along with minor details, in order to preserve the anonymity of those involved in this story.

Roddy Pinili grew up in the unremarkable middle-class town of Republic, Missouri, about 15 miles outside of Springfield. In 2004 Roddy’s stepdad, a servicemember in the Army, was called up to serve in Iraq; Roddy, then 16, and his mother, Amy, moved a few miles away to the nearby town of Clever, population 550, with a high school where he was the lone Filipino, among “a bunch of country farm boys.”

“This is where I got into trouble,” Roddy says. He started regularly driving back to Republic, a much bigger town, in search of friends. He met another Filipino kid there⁠—“He looked like me, and he shared the same interests as me”⁠—and he started picking up his new friend’s interests, too, which included breaking into vending machines. The boys were soon caught and Roddy spent 30 days in jail, with the scared teen vowing to his probation officer he’d never do anything like that again, and he didn’t.

After high school he got a job at a local Papa John’s, and through a friend there he met Maddy, born in Hungary, who’d immigrated to the U.S. with her mother and older sisters in the last days of the Soviet bloc. Like Roddy, she’d felt like something of an outsider in Missouri. They were married in 2015 and have four kids together: three boys and a girl, the youngest, who’s turning one this year.

Maddy had started to get her first sense of trouble just before the wedding, when it emerged that Roddy was missing a couple of official documents⁠—his birth certificate, for instance. Or a passport. Or a paper of any kind formally attesting to his status as a citizen of the United States. They wound up holding the ceremony in Arkansas, where document requirements were less stringent, but worries lingered. It wasn’t until Roddy showed up at the Springfield Motor Vehicle Department for a license renewal two years later that the penny finally dropped: Roddy was having trouble proving that he was an American citizen because he wasn’t one. Roddy wasn’t living legally in America at all.

Roddy’s mother, Amy, was born on the island of Luzon in the Philippines. When she was 18 she’d been introduced by friends to a U.S. Air Force pilot stationed at Clark Air Base, where they were married a year later and had a son.

Mount Pinatubo erupted on the island in June of 1991; Roddy, then age five, was evacuated with his mother to the U.S. along with more than 20,000 fellow refugees; the emergency nature of the evacuation meant that they entered the country without settled formal documents. Roddy’s father, a U.S. Air Force pilot, stayed behind at Clark as essential personnel. Roddy arrived in Missouri as the minor dependent of a humanitarian refugee. Like most of the refugees of the eruption of Mt. Pinatubo, he never returned to the Philippines.

In time his parents’ marriage broke up, and Amy remarried in 1999 to her second husband, also a serviceman. She became a legal permanent resident of the U.S. that same year. The obvious solution to Roddy’s unsettled citizenship was for his new stepdad to adopt him, but something always seemed to get in the way of the paperwork. And then, not too many months later, came September, 2001.

“We were in the middle of fixing immigration papers, and then it just stopped because of ⁹⁄₁₁,” Amy says, her accent a combination of Filipino pronunciations and a southern Missouri twang, like an immigration history preserved in speech. “And then as soon as a week or two later, they deployed. So my husband has to go to Kuwait.” For the next several years, Roddy’s stepfather flew Apache helicopters in Kuwait as the United States girded itself for the war against Iraq. By the time he returned to the U.S., this marriage, too, had broken up.

“I still get a headache for that one sadness,” Amy says. “That’s the big reason why Roddy is not adopted, and why we did not go citizen right away, like 10 or 20 years ago. The military thing, ⁹⁄₁₁. It stopped everything.”

Until that DMV visit it hadn’t occurred to Roddy that any of this might be a serious problem, but once it did, he moved quickly. He and Maddy hired a lawyer at a cost of $15,000, cobbled together from their savings and money borrowed from close friends, and prepared an application for permanent residence on the grounds of their marriage. Their lawyer wouldn’t even attend their U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services appointment with them.

At the appointment, the immigration officer saw that the couple had gotten their marriage license outside of their home state.

“Let’s just say he was old-school,” Roddy says. “Military drill sergeant, high and tight haircut. A hardass.” They explained that they’d been advised to go to Arkansas to get married.

“They always find a way,” the officer muttered.

“He trapped us in a corner,” Maddy recalls of the interview. “We didn’t have our attorney there, we didn’t know what to say.”

The officer presented Roddy with a pre-written statement attesting that Roddy had previously identified himself as a U.S. citizen. Until a few months earlier, he had never given the matter much thought; he’d arrived in the U.S. as a small child. In retrospect, he realizes the statement was a trap. “I have a sneaking suspicion the guy already knew he was wanting to deny us, and he was trying to figure out a way to do it.” Roddy signed the statement. His application was denied.

On the long car ride back to Springfield, Roddy kept to himself and didn’t say much. He was thinking about how thin the threads of his life were turning out to be, and how easily they could be cut. “God, make a way for us to get married and help with the paperwork,” he prayed silently. “God, make a way.”

A few weeks later, Roddy received a notice in the mail that the government was commencing removal proceedings against him. He could leave the country of his own volition and remain outside the U.S. for at least 10 years, or, if deported, never be eligible for U.S. residency at all. He would have the opportunity to plead his case before an immigration judge, but the hearing was set for March of 2020, just as Covid hit, and was put on hold. Eventually the hearing was rescheduled for December of this year. In the meantime, Roddy’s whole life⁠—his past, his future, his job, his security⁠—went into suspended animation. When the chance to settle his life woke up again, it was in a different America, and a different world.

Just a few years ago, things looked very different for immigrants like Roddy. In 2011, Democrats in the Senate reintroduced the long-dormant DREAM Act, which would have granted permanent legal residency to millions of undocumented immigrants who entered the country as minors. At the time, the proposal had widespread acceptance from the American public: 54 percent of Americans said in a Gallup poll that they supported the act, and 59 percent said they had a positive view of immigration generally. Barack Obama made the bill a centerpiece of his 2012 reelection campaign; when it failed, he replaced it through executive action with the DACA program, which met with broad and even bipartisan agreement that at least the notion of pluralism and of welcoming borders ought to be applauded, if only for the sake of appearances: eight years ago, then-Sen. Marco Rubio optimistically called on new president Donald Trump to champion the DREAM Act into law.

Republicans had been gnawing at the fearmongering rhetoric of “illegal immigrants” since Eisenhower’s day, but the notion persisted that America was a society built on a constant influx of new people and ideas, and that capturing the political loyalty of such new people was a path to continued electoral relevance. Both Ronald Reagan and George W. Bush made quixotic bids to open up a path to legal residence for undocumented immigrants, even as they postured for the cameras with tough talk about firm borders and strict law enforcement. Throughout these years, no group of immigrants could be more sympathetic or more fundamentally in tune with our national mythology than children who arrived and grew up in the U.S., with or without paperwork, wishing to become citizens of the nation that gave them refuge.

But there was an opposing current, too, and it ran just as deep. It began with the violent drive to expel German settlers from pre-revolutionary Pennsylvania. The same energy morphed into the Know Nothings of the 1850s, the America Firsters of the 1920s, and the John Birchers of the 1960s, fueled by America’s innate reticence to acknowledge class divisions openly. Its typical adherents were aspirational, anxiously climbing the ladder, but constantly fearful of any threat that could drag them back down. As historians Christopher Petrella and Ameer Hasan Loggins have argued, the very notion of a “middle class” in the U.S. is rooted in racial paranoia.

UC Berkeley assistant professor of sociology Stephanie Canizales conducted more than 200 interviews with child immigrants, which became the basis for her first book, Sin Padres, Ni Papeles: Unaccompanied Migrant Youth Coming of Age in the United States. Canizales calls Trump’s first term a “ticking time bomb.” Rather than the earlier understanding of child immigrants as a symbol of hope and potential for the American dream, Trump and his allies recast them as a kind of sleeper cell for the “Great Replacement”: a looming threat that would displace the established white majority middle class, if given a chance to grow up as citizens.

Immigrant children, she said, “will be undocumented adults eventually, is kind of how it was framed. I remember Jeff Sessions saying that unaccompanied children were coming to the U.S. as wolves in sheep’s clothing.”

Canizales met many of her young subjects when they were barely cognizant of their immigrant origins; even as the threat of deportation from the United States had become measurably greater, they struggled to adapt to the concept that they might be anything but American to begin with.

“I asked them, ‘Where do you see yourself in five years, in 10 years?’ And a lot of it was actually like, ‘I don’t really know,’ ” Canizales told me.

Roddy is 38 years old now. I first met him in April, a month after he had received his new date in immigration court and three months into the revived presidency of Donald Trump, and at that point he sounded more numb than scared. He had seen the delegates to the Republican National Convention hoisting signs reading “Mass Deportation Now”; the repeated references to immigrants as “not human” and “animals,”; the promises by Trump’s campaign team to “implement the most spectacular migration crackdown.”

That was agitating, but bearable. It was his family that made things tough. Roddy’s mother is a Trump supporter, and when he would sometimes raise his concerns with her about deportations and hostility to immigrants before the election, her responses had tended to be curt: “Trump is a man of God,” she told him, bewilderingly. And she remains unequivocal about this. “I have no problem whatsoever with what President Trump is doing,” she says. “It’s best for the country. It’s good. It’s why there is a border.”

She is deeply distressed by what’s happening to her son, but astonished that anyone would conflate him with the immigrants Trump was elected to deport. “He’s different,” she says. “He’s not illegal, and I’m not illegal. He didn’t break the border law. Someone did, but he didn’t. Those people down there, they just crossed with no permission.”

I asked her what she would do if the immigration court disagreed⁠—if, indeed, Roddy was deported and barred from returning. Amy paused before she answered. It seemed the first time she had considered the possibility. “Well, I have to listen to their reason,” she says. “I have to listen to why they can’t forgive him.”

Recently I got a text message with a new update: Maddy’s older sister Karen had been arrested by ICE. Karen, age five when she’d come over with her mother from Hungary, was not a U.S. citizen by birth like Maddy, and she had never filed the papers required to establish herself as a permanent resident. Karen’s boyfriend called Maddy’s parents only a few days ago to let them know that she had gone missing; they later learned through an attorney that she is being held somewhere in ICE detention, location unknown.

I asked Maddy how her parents were handling the crisis, and I was startled when she said they blamed it on Karen: Maddy’s parents are Trump supporters, too. “It’s her fault,” her father had told her. “She made bad choices.”

Maddy was stunned. “She’s your daughter,” was all she could say.

“I kept my mouth shut for a while, because I wanted to see how things would play out,” Maddy told me. “I don’t know much about politics; I was learning a lot from friends. I’m a homeschool mom, I’m stuck in my little world. But when they started explaining certain things and I started to see it and hear it for myself, I’m like, holy cow, we are being lied to. My parents are being lied to.”

In December, Roddy will go in for his long-awaited date before an immigration judge in Springfield. Roddy has hired a new attorney to represent him⁠—more capable, he hopes, than the last⁠—and they have prepared an appeal to cancel his removal on the grounds of extreme hardship and good moral character. But Roddy is under no illusions anymore. He knows that immigration judges are appointees of the executive branch, and that more than 100 of them have been fired since Donald Trump took office, to be replaced with administration loyalists. Out of the roughly 588,000 cases that have come before immigration courts in 2025 so far, more than 340,000 have ended in removal.

Roddy doesn’t know what he’ll do if that happens, or even where they’d send him, his Filipino citizenship notwithstanding. “I think the scariest thing for me is if he were in a detention center,” Maddy says. “We asked a nonprofit lawyer about that, and he said they were absolutely horrible. He’s like, ‘They will ship you around the United States. You won’t even know where they’re at.’ There’s absolutely no communication from the outside. Some people have been told, ‘Oh yeah, we’re sending you back to where you grew up.’ But then they ship them somewhere else, and they’re arrested there.”

Roddy has been wondering if he should “self-deport,” to use the administration’s jargon. But where? “I don’t know any of my language, Tagalog,” he says. “Absolutely no memories of my first five years in the Philippines. I don’t even have pictures. I don’t have contact with anybody there.” And there are other problems, too. Roddy has a Filipino passport, which he applied for at the outset of this process, but no one else in his family has one. They have children, and a house, and a job that Maddy is holding on to for everyone’s sake: Roddy hasn’t been able to work in the U.S. since his legal residency came into question. The future, any future, seems inconceivable.

In a better United States, the immigration judge would send Roddy and Maddy home smiling, and they would hug their children at the door of the Kansas City immigration building, and they would all drive out together to get a meal in celebration⁠—barbecued brisket and mac and cheese ladled over mashed potatoes, Roddy’s favorite.

But you and I know that this isn’t the America we live in anymore. In the America we live in, Roddy Pinili is very likely to be deported, or to emigrate voluntarily. The new year might well find him, with or without his family, making his way in an unknown country, in an unknown language.

“It’s a horrible feeling, having to live in fear,” Maddy said. “I just want this to be over. I don’t care. It’s like, at this point I could lose my husband.”
