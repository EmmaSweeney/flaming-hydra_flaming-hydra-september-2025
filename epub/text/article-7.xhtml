‘Frightened by the Sound of His Own Voice’

by Harry Siegel

Your favorite bits of profanity punctuating poetry? I’ve got:

“They fuck you up, your mum and dad” and “there is some shit I will not eat.”

That first poem has just the one curse, as its second word, and fuck, it penetrates. It ends not even a dozen lines later with the admonition that, and I’m quoting from memory here:

Man passes misery to man.
It deepens like a coastal shelf.
Get out as early as you can
And don’t have any kids yourself.

The second poem, not much longer, has Olaf, “a conscientious object,” thrown into a dungeon by the president after he’s duly notified of the yellowsonofabitch’s unpatriotic assertions, also including “I will not kiss your fucking flag.”

Olaf dies there, and this poem, too, concludes with death and a bit on what gets passed, perhaps, between generations:

Christ(of His mercy infinite)
i pray to see;and Olaf,too

preponderatingly because
unless statistics lie he was
more brave than me:more blond than you.

That led my poor popcorn jukebox brain to other lines and lists, about transmission and repetition, destiny and dignity, word-wielders wrestling with the ineffable.

There’s a bit from a noir—possibly jotted down somewhere inside this computer I’m typing on, or within the desk it sits atop, drawers filled with volumes of my often illegible scribblings, or maybe somewhere on the shelves with the page dogeared or the passage underlined or something about it scribbled in the margins inside one of the millions of pages heaped on jerryrigged shelves that might collapse and kill me some day in a patently ridiculous metaphor made manifest—from a noir, as I was saying, a bit about passion, mercy and justice I couldn’t quite remember, but kept itching at.

Searching for the line, Google’s artificial intelligence, whose results appear first now like it or not and I do not—spit back a result that matched the first few results below that:

“I have a secret passion for mercy. But justice is what keeps happening to people.”

I checked the “homework machine,” though (always check!) and that’s not quite right, though the autocompletion AI did correctly attribute the quote to The Goodbye Look by the great detective novelist Ross MacDonald, né (if that’s the word for it and I think it is) Kenneth Millar.

Here’s a digression worth noting as such to assure you, and me, that there is a path in mind it’s stepping away from:

One fairly unique thing about being a writer, for a man, can be having a maiden name so to speak.

When I got my first job as a journalist, at the New York Sun as it was being revived in 2002—seven decades after the first death of the paper that “shines for all”—the editor in chief, mindful of the revenant broadsheet’s long-forgotten traditions, and aligning those with his own long-cultivated newspaperman’s eccentricities, insisted I use the byline H.R. Siegel.

After a few months or so I took advantage of my role as a compositor as I laid out the pages—remember pages?—in Quark, and saved myself, as I thought of it at the time, from a lifetime as “H.R.” (and my colleague Ryan from a career as R.H.).

But John, a colleague whom I thought of as “Fipp,” his Gatsby-esque college nickname, remained “J.P.” long after I left the Sun—though that’s another story altogether about a newspaper (remember newspapers?) that’s now a website but one that still adheres to its superb and absurd style guide, carefully kept offline, with an entry for “I” that declares, and again I’m working from memory here, “An article may begin with ‘I’ only if the journalist has been shot in the groin,” and also insists that all spellings must match Webster’s excellent if increasingly anachronistic Second Edition published in 1934, a huge, heavy volume that sits in my office on a low shelf of reference books but nonetheless feels like it could be the one that does me in, natch.

In hindsight, bumping off H.R. was a poor choice for a face-blind fool like me who doesn’t remember half the people I knew or lives I’ve led, and who writes and speaks publicly often enough that strangers sometimes know my face or my voice. The anonymous initials would have forced people to help me figure out how they knew me, and whether or not I knew them.

When my now-teenaged daughter started her own scribbling, I was hopeful she might go by her initials, “J.R.,” rather than her given name, but I made the mistake of suggesting that to her so that’s out at least for now.

Returning now to The Goodbye Look, the correct quote there is so damn good, and even more beautiful in context if less easily clipped than the version AI substituted for it.

I wrote a long essay on Raymond Chandler for the Sun in maybe 2003 that’s since rotted off of the web, and the title of his final finished novel, 1953’s The Long Goodbye was surely echoing in the mind of Millar and then those of MacDonald’s avid readers when he published The Goodbye Look in in 1969, three years after Paul Newman played his detective on screen for the first time (renamed Harper rather than Archer for some stupid reason) and four years before Eliot Gould deconstructed Chandler’s private dick. The Goodbye Look was, like all of the Archer novels, in some sense the same book, each a beautifully wrought exploration of familial inheritance of original sins and how half-buried history burdens the young.

Maybe there’s no such thing as a complete passage, but the part interspersed here made me think of my wife and her fraught Southern California inheritances, and of her cousin and my dear friend Alex, who wrote a great review many years ago, in the more stilted than elevated style of the New York Times and since rotted off of the internet as best I can tell, about the food and service at the Bellevue cafeteria after re-depositing her mother at the mental hospital there, and of my good pal Dana wrestling with justice and mercy while working with and on behalf of gang kids at once against and stuck inside a system that can’t see them as kids.

Of the ones like us with our poor brains wrestling with what it means to live one life amid the excitement, and exhaustion, of other people’s lives and all the profound, profane poetry.
