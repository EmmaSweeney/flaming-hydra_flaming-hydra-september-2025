Death and the Hyrax

by Brian Hioe

Cape Hyrax, Namibia (Image: Charles J. Sharp [CC BY-SA 4.0] via Wikimedia Commons)

A fixation of mine over the past two years or so has been a creature called the hyrax. A small rat-like animal found in the Middle East and North Africa, it is actually a closer relative to the elephant and manatee than to any kind of rodent.

I don’t know when this obsession started. Just one day, hyraxes started appearing on my Instagram feed. My obsession before that had been hedgehogs. Over time I’ve found more and more hyrax pages start to emerge.

Again, guided by the invisible puppetmaster of the algorithm, a charming new animal has been elevated to a starring role in the popular parade. I remember when the internet collectively decided that raccoons were cute, for example. The hyrax craze may be the same.

The prominent Al-Jazeera reporter Anas al-Sharif was killed by an Israeli airstrike along with six other journalists the other day. A friend texted me in the morning that the last thing she had seen before going to sleep was a post by al-Sharif and that when she had woken up, the first thing she had seen on social media was his dead body. It was, of course, traumatizing for her.

And then the next thing she did was to forward me the image of his dead body, so that it was the first thing that I, too, saw in the morning.

“Hm, I shouldn’t have clicked on that reflexively,” I thought to myself, about one second later. Too late. That image, along with so many others like it, is just rattling around in my head now.

I know from my work as a journalist working on disinformation, and sometimes having to look at violent images, how they stay in your head forever. A very violent image never leaves. So you have to be careful with what you view. People who work professionally on this kind of material sometimes set up their computers so that they’ll have to click on an image to see it. That’s one way to have more agency over what enters your head.

More and more, am I haunted by the dualities of the internet. For close to two years now, sometimes it’s been a fun video of a hyrax that appears first on my Instagram feed, and sometimes it’s an image of someone being killed in Gaza. And yet, having never been anywhere near MENA, with a life primarily spent in the U.S. and Taiwan, it’s the internet that makes it possible for me to know all this at a great distance—life or death, hope or despair, suffering or cute animals.

It’s now possible for someone in Gaza to be burned alive in a video, and for the internet to dredge up their entire life histories in the span of an hour. People aren’t just being genocided “over there,” it’s part of all our lives—and if you post about it, families from all over the world will be appearing in your DMs begging for help.

Watching all of it stream by in a panoramic view, you realize how much any one thing has to compete for attention with all of the other atrocities and cat videos and political news going on in the world, as well.

Sometimes it’s strange to me that people view the modern internet and social media as purely an anodyne phenomenon—something that pacifies the masses, who just end up dancing to the tune of the algorithm, as they just stay bed-rotting glued to cute hyrax videos. But I think it’s a reaction to the early 2010s and the hopes many of us had for social media around the time of Occupy Wall Street and the Arab Spring, and how those hopes were so violently dashed on the rocks of reaction, and the ever tightening stranglehold of Big Tech.

Social media has created all these new ways for us to experience trauma, grief, and panic from a distance, too.

Committed activism involves strain on one’s mental health, in being aware of suffering; it always has. And yet you also have to manage the visceral trauma that is part of the job; that’s the only way to keep on going. Whether by connecting with friends, reading widely, writing, demonstrating, having a good breakfast, or deeply enjoying the antics of cute animals.
