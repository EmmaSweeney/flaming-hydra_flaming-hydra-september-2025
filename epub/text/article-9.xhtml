12 Sep 2025

Me and Emma Chamberlain Are Scared to Fly

By Amy Chu

My guilty pleasure is celebrity podcasts. I say “guilty” because I know I’m dependent on having a voice in my ear to drown out the anxieties that find me in the silent stretches of my day⁠—on my commutes, while I shower, before I fall asleep. I listen to one celebrity in particular, Emma Chamberlain, because we share an interest in fashion, though I do not feel represented by her.

To most any woman or girl under the age of thirty, Chamberlain requires no introduction. But if you need one, Chamberlain started out on YouTube in 2016. She was a normal 16-year-old girl who made videos about her daily life, including cooking in her mom’s apartment and her “hauls” from the thrift store and the dollar store. Though it started as a personal project to deal with a depressive episode, Chamberlain’s YouTube channel gained millions of subscribers as she became known for her quirky narration and fast-paced editing. A profile in Australian Vogue described her vlogging style as having the self-awareness of Phoebe Waller-Bridge and the self-deprecation of David Sedaris. The New York Times profiled her multiple times: first in 2019, again in 2023 when she decided to take a break from vlogging, and most recently in 2024, for her growing success outside of YouTube. She started her own coffee company, Chamberlain Coffee, became a brand ambassador for Louis Vuitton, and hosted Vogue’s red carpet interviews for the Met Gala five times. Chamberlain posts fewer YouTube videos these days, but she still connects with audiences via her popular Spotify-branded podcast, Anything Goes with Emma Chamberlain.

Despite having been dubbed “Gen-Z’s older sister,” Chamberlain is two years younger than me, and I consider myself Gen Z. We don’t share any traits of background, race, or class, but still, I’m sometimes struck by the similarity of our anxieties and wants. She manages a phone addiction. Like me, she hasn’t touched alcohol in months. Both her long-term relationship and mine ended in October 2023. As a fashion influencer, she’s touted shopping less, spending less, and being more sustainable⁠—values I share⁠—while her podcast regularly runs ads for Walmart and eBay. We’re both thinking about how to decorate our living room with the most “squishy comfortable” couch (hers was featured in Architectural Digest, mine on Facebook Marketplace). What endears this influencer to me most of all, though, is that she too hates flying.

If I am not her sister, friend, or fan, what is my relationship to Emma Chamberlain?

Every article suggests that Chamberlain is popular because of her relatable personality. She’s the it-girl influencer because she’s a refreshing departure from conventional social media culture.

Unlike many influencers-turned-celebrities, Chamberlain hasn’t pivoted into an acting or music career; she’s only expanded the purview of her online presence. Watching her forays into fashion’s most elite spaces feels similar to seeing a child in an art museum; their very presence changes the space, the art, and the interpretation of the rules around it all. In her 2023 Met Gala interview with musician Jack Harlow (which I’ve watched too many times) there’s a mesmerizing magic when the 20-year-old Emma tells Harlow to wait as she reapplies her makeup. Her viral reaction to his parting “Love ya!” is a charming break from the studiously indifferent cool girl persona you’d expect at a high-profile event like the Met Gala. What’s most astonishing is that it all happens on the red carpet, with the flawlessly-contoured Emma dressed in Louis Vuitton and a vintage Cartier tiara⁠—and she somehow remains relatable. Yarden Horwitz, a founder of the trend-forecasting company Spate, told the New York Times that “her authentic and ‘uncontroversial’ persona made her a go-to for brands⁠ ⁠… ‘Emma’s style has come to define the Gen Z look.’ ” If Chamberlain does not represent me personally, she has an undeniable effect on the culture and interests of my age group. Perhaps Emma is even more of a normal girl than I am?

That’s how I convince myself that it’s my duty as Gen-Zer to listen to her podcast. When I told my friend (who was born in 2001) that I had finally caught up to the zeitgeist by listening to Anything Goes with Emma Chamberlain, she visibly cringed. I can never win!

“Why?” my friend asked. “She doesn’t have a college degree.”

With this withering comment, my friend echoed the 2023 online backlash against Chamberlain’s superficial podcast observations, and her advice to listeners based on her own decision to not attend college⁠—which shows that celebrities influence culture, but only to the extent that audiences let them.

So Chamberlain rebranded. For a while, she dedicated about a third of her episodes to interviews with experts: fashion history with psychotherapists, mental health with physicians, general interviews with girly pop icons like Charli XCX and Kendall Jenner, all branded with the casual lowercase font that looks like a text message from a friend, “… i’m excited to be talking to dr. aditi nerurkar about stress.”

Admittedly, I don’t listen to any of those podcast episodes. In my opinion, Chamberlain is not a good interviewer; she doesn’t ask interesting questions. It’s not the type of escapist content I seek out when I’m feeling like I need a dose of the internet. Plus, I feel like I should distance myself from Chamberlain for reasons beyond her being out of touch or unqualified. For all the support she’s gotten for her authenticity, I also wonder how she upholds the less-admirable parts of the status quo. Should I be “consuming content” from creators with less exposure? Should I be listening to some educational podcast about climate change instead?

My biggest gripe with Emma might be that she uses the word “doomscrolling” incorrectly. She brings it up often, because Emma is honest about managing her internet addiction. “​​You know what doomscrolling is? It’s when you scroll on TikTok or Instagram for hours and hours and hours and hours and consume hours and hours and hours and hours’ worth of useless content just because you don’t have the self-control to turn it off.”

Doomscrolling is an Internet term thought to have been invented by Ashik Siddique in 2018 and popularized in 2020, when one could not scroll Twitter without being bombarded with headlines of covid death rates and videos of police brutality, delivered at an overwhelming rate by the internet. Chamberlain doesn’t mention this dimension of it, though she has said, “I think my Internet addiction was a little bit worse and more toxic because my job is on the Internet.”

Teens today are no less reliant on and plagued by online spaces than are those who profit from them professionally. Fellow Hydra Brian Hioe points out that only on the internet can an adorable animal video exist immediately adjacent to a photo of the bodies of journalists assassinated in Gaza. I’d also argue that Gen Z’s usage of technology goes beyond pure consumption. Many of us treat social media as a key resource for creation and connection. Still, Emma’s stated aversion to her workplace mirrors a growing opinion among young people that social media is bad for their peers, though not necessarily bad for themselves. According to the Pew Research Center, “About half of teens (48%) say social media platforms have a mostly negative effect on people their age. Meanwhile, 14% see a mostly negative impact for themselves, up slightly from 9% in 2022.” Girls, especially, are cutting back on phone usage.

At the beginning of 2025, Anything Goes with Emma Chamberlain underwent another rebrand. Dropping the polished image of Chamberlain reflected in a hand mirror, the podcast logo returned to a hand-doodled portrait of a tired-looking Emma in a scraggly pixie cut. The updated description reads, “emma prefers to share her thoughts with a microphone rather than a physical human being⁠ ⁠…”

Now Chamberlain alternates between advice sessions, fashion opinions, and personal opinions on topics like mental health and relationships. As far as research goes, she limits her efforts to pulling up a Webster’s Dictionary definition or reporting back on her Google search results. She has admitted in a few episodes that the old format, interviews with experts, did not feel true to her podcast’s original goal: to help people feel less alone.

My feeling of closeness to Emma Chamberlain wavers based on what else is happening in the world. It’s difficult to hear about her vacations while Israel starves over half a million people in Gaza. Yet the same could be said about me. If I turned off my phone and deactivated my social media, I could easily enjoy the comforts of my life in complete ignorance of the news in Palestine, which is often posted directly to my feed by Palestinian people, including children, and by journalists.

After flying back from my Banff vacation this weekend, I watched Emma’s newest YouTube vlog, facing my fear of flying (alone). She voices over a clip of herself squirming in her seat during takeoff and landing:

I’ve always been an anxious person since I was a child. But as a child, I wasn’t afraid of flying. Even though I was an anxious kid, I had this sort of beautiful delusion, this blissful ignorance that prevented a lot of anxieties for me. This delusion and blissful ignorance made me feel like rare tragedies couldn’t happen to me. But it seems the older I get, the more terrified I become of the general dangers of day-to-day life, the firmer I grasp that bad things could happen to me or to anyone I love at any time. And this is a reality that has been very challenging for me to accept.

Most of the comments on the episode are positive, but a few veer into contempt: “I feel like if I flew first class, most of my anxiety would go away⁠ ⁠…” It’s true. Privilege and the pleasures of consumption can help mitigate our anxieties, in much the same way that I can choose to listen to podcasts to tune out the news.

But even if I flew first class, I think I’d still hate flying. My fear heightened this year after the DC plane crash. I wonder if Emma’s plane anxiety worsened, as mine did, after the administration laid off air traffic controllers. I wonder if news of the murder of 25-year-old Fatma Hassonah, a photojournalist, made it to her Instagram feed? Where was Emma when ICE touched down in her home city of Los Angeles, and the city fought back? I may never know for sure because Emma Chamberlain does not discuss politics, at least not so far⁠—but she does talk about her anxieties.

In the Up and Up newsletter on Gen-Z culture and politics, Rachel Janfaza recently wrote, “No generation is monolithic. And yet, there [is] a set of shared global circumstances across any generation’s life span that shape[s] how they collectively view the world.” In any humanitarian or natural disaster, women suffer greater and longer consequences. Even as we confront the widening gaps across class and citizenship, young women’s realities, mine and Emma’s along with millions of others, are defined by the same forces: capitalism, war, patriarchy. Perhaps what makes us normal girls is not that we suffer similarly or equally, but how we worry. We know we are not immune to airplanes and bombs falling out of the sky, and the internet has brought us closer together than ever.
