02 Sep 2025

The Past Is What You Make of It

by Ian Williams

I was at a bar with some younger friends recently and we talked about movies we’d been watching. I mentioned offhandedly that I appreciated the mid-budget films of yesteryear, and summer comedies, and that the disappearance of both those categories was a cultural loss. I got looks. That I was wearing a flat cap as I shared these views was, I was told, especially funny. Because really my problem was that I was old and nostalgic.

Early this month, on Bluesky, I saw an animated back and forth over whether the internet is getting worse. It was mostly a “forth,” since most agreed yes, the internet has always sucked so why even have the debate? Smart people whom I like and admire thought it obvious that being nostalgic for the pre-Web 2.0 internet is a foolish thing to be. Did you know (did you know I bet you didn’t) that the internet was created by the United States defense industry?

After David Lynch died I spent some time reading obituaries and revisiting some analyses of his work. I was surprised to see so many critics praising his transcendence of “hollow nostalgia” and “the sentimental commonplace.” Because the message was clear: nostalgia is terrible and there was one person in American pop culture who cataloged it correctly, unlike everyone else.

Nostalgia, the bête noire of politics, the black hole of culture⁠ ⁠… The worst thing of all.

But because things right now are demonstrably, measurably getting worse, in most ways, for most people, the dance on nostalgia’s corpse dehistoricizes our moment. The climate is collapsing, the human rights of marginalized groups are being actively rolled back, people can’t afford basic goods or housing, and the cultural industries have been almost entirely hollowed out.

The basic charge against nostalgia is twofold. One, things have always been bad⁠—and true as that is, it fixes “bad” as an eternal, even an essential quality, and history itself as Sisyphean. The long arc of history bends from somewhere terrible towards an ever-unattainable justice, forever. But history didn’t end and maybe it doesn’t bend toward justice, or at least, as JB Pritzker recently said, it doesn’t, unless you bend it yourself.

Two, some things were better, for whom? Up until just a few months ago, LGBTQ+ rights were far better than they were even 20 years ago. Black popular culture is woven more openly into the fabric of daily American life than at any point in our history. Women go to school at higher rates than men, and the pernicious wage gap is somewhat less grotesque than in past decades. But wait, no, things are worse than they were five years ago, and getting worse by the moment, all those gains are on the brink, and the chuds running things now are threatening not only to reverse every step forward made by this society on behalf of marginalized people, but to hurl the U.S. back to the nineteenth century. There’s every chance that in 2035 we’ll be nostalgic for 2015, and for good reason.

People understand history through culture and media. Dumb videos about the nostalgia a Gen Xer feels for a television show are almost always about something bigger. Maybe it’s the notion that everyone watched the same show at the same time, allowing for easy socialization at the office water-coolers of the olden time. Maybe the simplicity of an unquestioningly shared moral universe, however illusory, was comforting. Maybe the visibility of working middle-class actors allowed for a little aspiration between drudgeries. They might resent their seven streaming subscriptions! Or they might miss episodic television series that lasted for years and years existing at all. All of those things are conditions of possibility we’ve lost, we know we’ve lost them, and that’s worth being sad about.

The late cultural theorist Svetlana Boym posited two forms of nostalgia, the restorative and the reflective. She developed these categories in The Future of Nostalgia (2001), her best-known work, a book about a trip to the former Soviet bloc. Her motivation was to try to figure out why people were still nostalgic for the culture and rhythms of a communist way of life that was never coming back.

Boym’s restorative nostalgia is the bad nostalgia, seeking to resurrect the past at the expense of the present and future: “Restorative nostalgia does not think of itself as nostalgia, but rather as truth and tradition.” This is the nostalgia of the far right, or the “Real America” that U.S. Republicans have been peddling since the time of Reagan: things would go back to the ’80s (or the ’50s, or the ’20s) if we just kept all these OTHER people quiet, if we got rid of them. Or killed them, even. Restorative nostalgia is the nostalgia of Confederate statues and AI slop. It’s a paralysis of memory, a stasis that threatens to overwhelm you, and it’s one bad economy away from breaking out.

Reflective nostalgia is different. It lingers in the past, sifting through the ruins for what might be brought forward in productive ways. “Instead of recreation of the lost home,” Boym writes, “reflective nostalgia can foster the creation of aesthetic individuality.” It’s also sad. Melancholy. Because there are only a few useful things left to bring home, and when you return (you must always return) you might as easily feel a heavy weight in your chest as you do the thrum of memory, and of camaraderie, across the decades.

You linger. You can’t help it. And then you carry on.

Each and every instance of nostalgia contains both restorative and reflective aspects, even if it might shade heavily toward one side or the other; it’s a spectrum, not a binary. And that’s why our readings of nostalgia are so garbled. We’re on guard against restorative nostalgia⁠—and rightfully so, given that the U.S. president has announced a review of the collections at the Smithsonian, with the intention of removing “improper ideology,” and also freaked out over a fucking Cracker Barrel rebranding⁠—so much so that we pick up on every atom of the bad nostalgia, even in the things which are, on balance, far more on the reflective side.

Now is not the only thing that’s real. When a young union organizer consults unionization literature from the ’50s or gets tips from a labor veteran, even though Fordism is never coming back, that’s nostalgic. When John Ganz, analyzing Martin Scorsese’s comments about “content,” states that maybe we’ve messed up our sensemaking in the death of form, that’s nostalgic. When a Gen Xer openly misses grunge rock when what they really miss is a world where un-corporate-packaged, weird, unexpected, idiosyncratic artists could break big out of nowhere, a world where even smaller musical acts could make a living, that’s nostalgic.

The idea that if Kurt Cobain were still alive, and Nirvana re-formed, then all of this annoying prefab pop music would disappear? That’s restorative. It can’t happen. You can’t go back. Recognizing that the world before the Telecommunications Act, the rise of Spotify, and radio consolidation allowed for a greater variety of popular music, though, is reflective. When someone says “they don’t make real rock music anymore,” they’re maybe saying they miss Nirvana, they hate current music, and that there was once a world of relative security if you wanted to start a weird band. All these ideas exist in the same short statement.

The internet always contained the seeds of its current state, but it really was better before social media and algorithmic governance. It was better when people read rather than watched. It was better when that middle ground of art⁠—neither hand-to-mouth indie nor gauche blockbuster⁠—was viable. It was better when college, food, and housing were all affordable. It was better before venture capital and private equity infiltrated every single aspect of commerce, I’m sorry, it simply was, and to insist, despite all evidence, that everything was exactly as bad or worse than it is now? Some (though not all) of those who experienced it all firsthand will consult their own memories and tell you plainly, no, it’s not true; the internet was so much better before the billionaires fucked it all up.

There are things worth being nostalgic for. To fold all past conditions into the same stupid, negatively polarized, distinctly U.S. binary is getting maddening, in a time of precipitous decline like the one we’re living through now. It’s how you get Hakeem Jefferies and Chuck Schumer instead of FDR or even Eisenhower, and Clarence Thomas instead of Thurgood Marshall. It’s how you cede the argument that some things might’ve been better, and are worth picking back up, to retrograde dummies who insist that all things were better (all contains within it the category of some⁠—but none does not).

You sift through the ruins. You find the artifacts. You translate the texts. It can be sad work and it might drive you crazy. But there’s still gold in those ruins and spells in those texts, and they’re still worth finding.
