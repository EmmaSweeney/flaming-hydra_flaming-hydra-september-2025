05 Sep 2025

Serving Some Purpose

by Talia Lavin and Zac h Rabiroff

A conversation about art and authoritarianism

Zach Rabiroff: Talia, I’ve been thinking lately about Bertolt Brecht, and what he said about the trees.

Brecht was a refugee from the Nazis and living in Stockholm in 1939, when he wrote a poem called “To Future Generations.” The question he had on his mind concerned the weight of the world in that moment: How much responsibility did an artist have to talk or think about something, or anything, else? Just what was a poet supposed to be doing at a time like this?

What are these times, when
A conversation about trees is almost a crime
Because it implies silence about so many misdeeds,
When, if you’re calmly crossing the street,
It means your friends can’t reach you
Who are in need?

And I’ve been thinking about that now, here in the United States in 2025, because I’m wondering the same thing.

Talia Lavin: That Brecht poem is really lovely, and strikes at the heart of the question. I’ve always been inspired by art from ages of authoritarianism. Osip Mandelstam is my favorite poet, and he was arrested for writing a poem mocking Stalin, and reading it at a private party. He was arrested in 1933 for that poem. He thought he was going to be killed.

The Stalin Epigram

Our lives no longer feel ground under them.
At ten paces you can’t hear our words.

But whenever there’s a snatch of talk
it turns to the Kremlin mountaineer

the ten thick worms his fingers,
his words like measures of weight,

the huge laughing cockroaches on his top lip,
the glitter of his boot-rims.

Ringed with a scum of chicken-necked bosses
he toys with the tributes of half-men.

One whistles, another meows, a third snivels.
He pokes out his finger and he alone goes boom.

He forges decrees in a line like horseshoes,
One for the groin, one the forehead, temple, eye.

He rolls the executions on his tongue like berries.
He wishes he could hug them like big friends from home.

Mandelstam didn’t die of this poem. But he was arrested, and exiled, and then arrested again during the Great Purge in 1937, and he died of typhoid fever in a transit camp before he turned 50. The author of Tristia and the Voronezh Notebooks was buried in a mass grave, and it started with this poem.

ZR: It’s an incredible poem. And it’s a familiar kind of fantasy, for writers: that we’ll have a shot at our own Edward R. Murrow moment one day, when we can speak an important truth to the public, and to history.

A fantasy maybe increasingly out of reach. There are fewer people reading fewer outlets now, and too many of those who remain no longer have a confidently shared faith in objective reality to begin with. It’s a problem! So we have to imagine that whatever we’re writing about is serving some purpose, or some imagined audience that’s smaller even than the ones Mandelstam was writing for—just a little private party, even if he knew he’d be betrayed. On some level, we have to be writing for ourselves.

TL: Mandelstam’s audience was the NKVD, really. And the head of that terrible agency memorized it, apparently. Reading Auden or Mandelstam or Tsvetaeva or Akhmatova on authoritarianism is so inspiring, even when it’s bleak. The Russians at least were writing for posterity—“for the drawer”—unpublishably, at that moment. And maybe there’s value in that? Of course there is. My heroes: Platonov, Bulgakov. The Master and Margarita was a drawer novel, because it mocked the Soviet literati. For decades. And it’s a masterpiece. Joseph Brodsky, the poet, was put on trial for writing his poems. And at his trial in 1964—when the gulag was still alive but they were institutionalizing enemies of the regime in insane asylums, because rejecting Marxism-Leninism was an official mental disease—he said this:

Judge: And, in general, what is your specific occupation?

Brodsky: Poet. Poet-translator.

Judge: And who said you’re a poet? Who ranked you among poets?

Brodsky: No one. (Unsolicited) Who ranked me as a member of the human race?

Judge: Did you study for this?

Brodsky: Study for what?

Judge: To become a poet. Did you attend some university where people are trained  … where they’re taught …?

Brodsky: I didn’t think it was a matter of education.

Judge: How, then?

Brodsky: I think that  … (perplexed) it comes from God  …


It comes from God. He was sentenced to five years of hard labor. He shoveled shit and read Auden in exile, this disciple of Akhmatova.

ZR:There were many dissident poets in her orbit, and many of them were outright bastards. Nikolai Gumilev, for instance. Under any other historical circumstance, a man like Gumilev—an inveterate reactionary, a full-bore apologist for right-wing aristocracy, and an opponent of socialist reform—might have been remembered by posterity as a talented but objectionable reactionary crank; the sort of playwright and poet you have to teach with an asterisk in college curricula.

But the circumstances altered both the man and the memory. When Gumilev’s Moscow theater opened its season in 1922, the year after the poet was executed on the orders of Vladimir Lenin, audience members called for Gumilev’s work to be performed, and the theater was shut down: not because they cared passionately for his politics or even his poetry, but because he had come to symbolize the notion that art as a personal statement of expression can transcend the judgments of the state. An idea, once born, cannot be killed, nor can it be controlled. This is the thought that may have the power to haunt autocrats when they lie awake at night. So, the legend went, when Gumilev was led off to his execution by the NKVD, he was seen to have smiled to himself and laughed.

So let’s say there’s value in speaking out—and I do think there is. That raises the question of what it is we’re obliged to be saying. One answer is just to observe the world as we see it, and try to set it down that same way, “for the drawer.” But there’s another approach, maybe just as important. Milan Kundera has a line: “The struggle of man against nature is the struggle of memory against forgetting.”

I get the sense these days that all of us in the United States, whether we know it or not, are already refugees. The country we knew isn’t here anymore except to the extent that we once knew it, and if anyone is going to remember what it meant or what it could have been, it will only be because the writers and poets and other artists among us now find a way to tell them.

TL: When I think about being American these days, the book I keep circling back to is Svetlana Alexievich’s Second-Hand Time, about the dislocation of identity in the post-Soviet USSR. I guess you can tell I focused on Russian lit in college! But it’s a great book. Some grasp the collapse eagerly, some are totally at sea, there’s a rash of suicides she writes about... it’s a book of interviews and it’s complex and shattering. Alexievich is astounding that way: she lets people talk, and crystallizes what they say into diamond, beautiful and sharp enough to cut through anything. You are instantly transported into the stories, into the pain she listens to and transmits to the page like it’s a sacred act.

ZR:Gore Vidal, one of my Extremely Problematic Faves, wrote a line in the wonderful novel Julian: “How marvelous books are, crossing worlds and centuries, defeating ignorance and, finally, cruel time itself.”

Which is to say, maybe we’re setting down words in the hope that somewhere, in some place, there’s going to be a person whose fear looks like our fear, and whose hopes looks like our hopes, and by showing them what we saw and felt they can feel some connection, and maybe get some ideas.

TL: Mandelstam has a poem about feeling unequal to his times. It goes in part:

The wolfhound century leaps on my back
but I have no wolf in my blood,
oh hide me deep and warm, like a cap
in the sleeve of Siberia’s coat.

Let me see no coward, no sticky slime,
no wheel with bones and blood,
but silver foxes that shine all night
with a grace from before the flood.

Bear me off to the dark-flowing Yenisey
where pine trees stretch to the stars,
because I have no wolf in my blood,
and shall only be killed by my peers.

Still, he wrote his “Stalin Epigram.” Mocked the moustache and the cruelty alike. Maybe you don’t have to feel brave or worthy to write brave and worthy things in the wolfhound century.
